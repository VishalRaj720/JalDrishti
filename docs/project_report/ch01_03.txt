# 1. INTRODUCTION

Groundwater is the main source of drinking water in Jharkhand. Most villages and many towns depend on hand pumps, dug wells and bore wells. Jharkhand is a hard-rock state. Outside the Damodar valley coalfields and the river plains, groundwater is stored in a thin weathered layer near the surface and in cracks (fractures) in the hard rock below it. The Central Ground Water Board (CGWB) describes this as a three-layer system: a weathered zone about 13 to 30 m deep, a fractured zone that ends somewhere between 90 m and 260 m depending on the district, and solid rock below that [@naquim; @cgwb_booklet]. The water table also moves up and down with the seasons. In the CGWB record used in this project, the median depth to water across the state is 7.20 m in May and 3.22 m in August [@cgwb_levels].

The Singhbhum Shear Zone, in the East Singhbhum and Saraikela-Kharsawan districts, contains India’s main uranium deposits: Jaduguda, Bhatin, Narwapahar, Turamdih, Banduhurang, Mohuldih and Bagjata [@udepo]. These are mined by the Uranium Corporation of India Ltd. using conventional underground and open-pit methods [@tripathi2008]. The ore is uraninite in metamorphic rock, found together with sulphide minerals such as pyrite and chalcopyrite, in what is a uranium–copper–iron deposit [@pal2013; @pal2011]. None of these mines uses in-situ recovery.

In-situ recovery (ISR), also called in-situ leaching, is a different way of mining uranium. Instead of digging out the ore, wells are drilled into the ore body. A chemical solution called the *lixiviant* is injected through some wells. In the alkaline type of ISR, the lixiviant is local groundwater with added oxygen and carbonate. It dissolves the uranium, and the uranium-rich solution is pumped back to the surface through other wells. The operator pumps out slightly more water than it injects (this extra is called the *bleed*) so that groundwater flows inward and the solution stays inside the wellfield. ISR supplied 56% of the uranium mined in the world in 2022 [@wna]. It is used where the ore lies in porous, water-bearing sand or sandstone that is sealed by low-permeability layers above and below [@wna].

ISR does not only move uranium. The lixiviant also raises sulphate and total dissolved solids (TDS) in the groundwater, releases radium-226, and, depending on the ore, can mobilise arsenic, selenium and molybdenum [@usgs_tx; @hall2009; @saunders2016]. If the solution escapes past the ring of monitoring wells around the wellfield, the event is called an *excursion*. After mining ends, the aquifer must be *restored* by pumping and cleaning the water, and then watched for many years. In the United States, excursions are detected by regularly sampling the monitoring wells and comparing a set of indicator chemicals (such as chloride, conductivity and alkalinity) with control limits. Uranium itself is not used as an indicator, because it is slowed down by the rock and arrives much later than the other chemicals [@nureg1569]. In other words, excursions are found by manual sampling, after they have already happened.

This project was carried out under the UDAAN UG Fellowship Programme (2025-26) of the TEXMiN–BIT Sindri CPS Centre of Excellence, with the title “Smart Water Monitoring: Machine Learning and CPS for Safe & Sustainable Mining”. The proposal aimed to use machine learning (ML) and cyber-physical systems (CPS) to assess water-quality damage and aquifer vulnerability in ISR mining areas, to find gaps in the existing monitoring data, and to build a prototype decision-support tool.

One fact shapes the whole project: **no ISR uranium mine operates in Jharkhand, and none is planned.** Every mining site in this project is therefore hypothetical. Every modelled result means only this: *“if ISR-strength solution entered this aquifer at this point, this is how it would behave.”* It is never a judgement that mining is feasible, never a plan, and never a permit. The project is a preparedness screening study. It tries to answer, in advance, how far and how fast contamination would spread, whether it could reach the shallow drinking-water layer, which blocks and residents should be informed, and what should be monitored and where.

The system built in this project is called **JalDrishti** (“a view of water”). It has six main parts:

1. A groundwater contamination model based on standard analytical equations, adapted for Jharkhand’s fractured hard rock and fed with real Jharkhand data and real ISR operating records from Texas, USA.
2. A machine learning model trained on the output of this physics model, which gives fast answers together with calibrated uncertainty bands.
3. An assessment of 397 government wells against the Indian drinking-water standard IS 10500:2012 [@is10500].
4. An alert system that informs residents by email when a limit is crossed or a screening is published.
5. A ranking of all 264 blocks of the state by how poorly they are monitored.
6. A web portal with five user roles, deployed on the internet.

The way of working changed during the ten months of the fellowship. The first five months were spent building the data platform and training ML models on measured and synthetic water-quality data, as the proposal described. In June 2026 it became clear that these models could not show where contamination would travel, because the measured data had no information about spread over distance or time. The prediction part was therefore rebuilt around a physics-based transport model, with ML trained on its output. The last three months turned this into a working product, tested it in several review rounds, and deployed it. This report describes the work in the order the template asks for: objectives, problem statement, methodology, tools, month-wise work, results, prototype details, challenges, expenditure, conclusion and future scope.

# 2. OBJECTIVES

The primary objective of this project is to design and develop a groundwater contamination screening and decision-support system for Jharkhand that can estimate how contamination from a hypothetical ISR uranium operation would spread, check the existing groundwater quality record, identify monitoring gaps, and inform the people concerned.

The three objectives given in the fellowship proposal were:

- **O1:** Develop ML-based predictive models to assess water quality degradation and aquifer vulnerability in mining-affected ISR regions.
- **O2:** Identify key data gaps in hydrogeological systems and recommend improved monitoring strategies.
- **O3:** Design a prototype tool that integrates prediction, visualization and decision-support features for stakeholders.

Based on these, the specific objectives of the project are as follows:

1. To collect the available groundwater data for Jharkhand (water quality, water levels, aquifer maps, geology, rivers and uranium deposits) and organise it in one spatial database.
2. To build a contamination transport model that estimates how far and how fast chemicals from a hypothetical ISR operation would spread, using real Jharkhand hydrogeology and real ISR records from Texas.
3. To develop machine learning models that give fast predictions of the plume footprint, travel distance, concentration at the monitoring ring and chance of an excursion, each with an uncertainty band.
4. To check whether contamination at ore depth could reach the shallow aquifer from which people drink.
5. To assess the existing measured groundwater quality against IS 10500:2012 and show the results block by block, keeping measured results separate from modelled ones.
6. To identify the gaps in hydrogeological and water-quality monitoring, and recommend where new monitoring is needed most.
7. To design an alert system that informs officials and residents, with a clear explanation and a recommended action for every alert.
8. To develop a prototype web portal with role-based access for administrators, regulators, analysts, field officers and residents.
9. To deploy the system online and test it thoroughly.
10. To state the limits of the system clearly, so that a modelled estimate is never mistaken for a measurement and a missing measurement is never shown as “safe”.

# 3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

Mining regions in India depend on groundwater for drinking water, and monitoring of that groundwater is mostly manual. Samples are collected at intervals, tested in a laboratory and compared with limits. As the fellowship proposal pointed out, this approach reacts to problems after they happen instead of predicting them.

For ISR uranium mining, this problem is sharper. ISR has never been carried out in India, so there is no Indian data on how an ISR leak would behave. If ISR were ever considered for the Singhbhum uranium belt, officials would have no tool to judge where contamination could go, how long it would take to reach drinking-water wells, which villages would be affected, or where monitoring wells should be placed. The existing international experience comes from sandstone aquifers in the USA [@nureg1569; @usgs_tx], while Jharkhand’s uranium lies in fractured hard rock, where water and chemicals move very differently.

The existing monitoring data also has serious limits. The 2023 CGWB water-quality record for Jharkhand has only one sample per well, taken in one year, with no repeat samples [@cgwb_gwq]. No well depth is recorded. Iron and arsenic were never tested at any of the 397 wells, and manganese has no column at all. In the three Singhbhum districts, which make up the uranium belt, 55 wells were sampled but never tested for uranium. With data like this, a purely data-driven ML model cannot forecast water-quality trends or show how a plume would spread, because nothing in the data ever spread. Most published groundwater ML studies need long records from many stations [@sahoo2013; @rajaee2019]. Standard vulnerability indices such as DRASTIC need soil, recharge and other layers that are not available at a useful scale for the state [@aller1987], and they answer a different question: they describe pollution from the surface, not from an injection deep underground.

Finally, even when a limit is crossed, the information often does not reach the people who drink the water. A result that sits in a database table, visible only to someone who logs in and searches for it, does not protect anyone.

**Problem statement:** There is no tool for Jharkhand that can (a) estimate the spread of contamination from a hypothetical ISR operation in fractured hard rock using real local data, with honest uncertainty; (b) show what the existing groundwater quality record says, and what it does not say; (c) identify the places where monitoring is weakest; and (d) inform the officials and residents concerned through a working alert system. This project aims to build such a tool, and to be clear about which of these questions the available data can and cannot answer.
