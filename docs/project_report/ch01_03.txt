# 1. INTRODUCTION

Groundwater is the main source of drinking water in Jharkhand, drawn from hand pumps, dug wells and bore wells. Jharkhand is a hard-rock state: outside the coalfields and river plains, groundwater is stored in a thin weathered layer near the surface and in cracks (fractures) in the rock below it. The Central Ground Water Board (CGWB) describes this as a three-layer system: a weathered zone about 13 to 30 m deep, a fractured zone that ends somewhere between 90 m and 260 m depending on the district, and solid rock below that [@naquim; @cgwb_booklet]. The median depth to water across the state is 7.20 m in May and 3.22 m in August [@cgwb_levels].

The Singhbhum Shear Zone, in the East Singhbhum and Saraikela-Kharsawan districts, contains India’s main uranium deposits: Jaduguda, Bhatin, Narwapahar, Turamdih, Banduhurang, Mohuldih and Bagjata [@udepo]. The ore is uraninite in metamorphic rock with sulphides such as pyrite and chalcopyrite [@pal2013; @pal2011], mined by the Uranium Corporation of India Ltd. underground and in open pits [@tripathi2008]; none uses in-situ recovery.

In-situ recovery (ISR), also called in-situ leaching, is a different way of mining uranium. Instead of digging out the ore, wells are drilled into the ore body. A chemical solution called the *lixiviant* is injected through some wells. In the alkaline type of ISR, the lixiviant is local groundwater with added oxygen and carbonate. It dissolves the uranium, and the uranium-rich solution is pumped back to the surface through other wells. The operator pumps out slightly more water than it injects (this extra is called the *bleed*) so that groundwater flows inward and the solution stays inside the wellfield. ISR supplied 56% of the uranium mined in the world in 2022 [@wna]. It is used where the ore lies in porous, water-bearing sand or sandstone that is sealed by low-permeability layers above and below [@wna].

ISR does not only move uranium. The lixiviant also raises sulphate and total dissolved solids (TDS) in the groundwater, releases radium-226, and, depending on the ore, can mobilise arsenic, selenium and molybdenum [@usgs_tx; @hall2009; @saunders2016]. If the solution escapes past the ring of monitoring wells around the wellfield, the event is called an *excursion*. After mining ends, the aquifer must be *restored* by pumping and cleaning the water, and then watched for many years. In the United States, excursions are detected by regularly sampling the monitoring wells for indicator chemicals (such as chloride, conductivity and alkalinity); uranium is not an indicator, because the rock slows it and it arrives much later [@nureg1569]. Excursions are therefore found by manual sampling, after they have happened.

This project was carried out under the UDAAN UG Fellowship Programme (2025-26) of the TEXMiN–BIT Sindri CPS Centre of Excellence, with the title “Smart Water Monitoring: Machine Learning and CPS for Safe & Sustainable Mining”. The proposal aimed to use machine learning (ML) and cyber-physical systems (CPS) to assess water-quality damage and aquifer vulnerability in ISR mining areas, to find gaps in the existing monitoring data, and to build a prototype decision-support tool.

One fact shapes the whole project: **no ISR uranium mine operates in Jharkhand, and none is planned.** Every mining site in this project is therefore hypothetical. Every modelled result means only: *“if ISR-strength solution entered this aquifer at this point, this is how it would behave”*; it is never a judgement that mining is feasible, a plan or a permit. The project is a preparedness screening study: how far and how fast would contamination spread, could it reach the shallow drinking-water layer, who should be informed, and what should be monitored where?

The system built in this project is called **JalDrishti** (“a view of water”). It has six main parts:

1. A groundwater contamination model based on standard analytical equations, adapted for fractured hard rock and fed with real Jharkhand data and Texas ISR records.
2. A machine learning model trained on the physics model’s output, giving fast answers with calibrated uncertainty bands.
3. An assessment of 397 government wells against the Indian drinking-water standard IS 10500:2012 [@is10500].
4. An alert system that informs residents by email when a limit is crossed or a screening is published.
5. A ranking of all 264 blocks of the state by how poorly they are monitored.
6. A web portal with five user roles, deployed on the internet.

The first five months followed the proposal and trained ML models on measured and synthetic water-quality data. In June 2026 these models were found unable to show where contamination would travel, and the prediction part was rebuilt around a physics-based transport model with ML trained on its output (Section 9.3). The remaining months turned this into a tested, deployed product, with the model inputs in the uranium belt taken from the belt’s own measurements.

# 2. OBJECTIVES

The primary objective is a groundwater contamination screening and decision-support system for Jharkhand that estimates how contamination from a hypothetical ISR operation would spread, checks the groundwater quality record, identifies monitoring gaps and informs the people concerned.

The three objectives given in the fellowship proposal were:

- **O1:** Develop ML-based predictive models to assess water quality degradation and aquifer vulnerability in mining-affected ISR regions.
- **O2:** Identify key data gaps in hydrogeological systems and recommend improved monitoring strategies.
- **O3:** Design a prototype tool that integrates prediction, visualization and decision-support features for stakeholders.

The specific objectives were:

1. To collect Jharkhand’s groundwater data (quality, levels, aquifers, geology, rivers, uranium deposits) into one spatial database.
2. To build a transport model of how far and how fast chemicals from a hypothetical ISR operation would spread, using real Jharkhand hydrogeology and Texas ISR records.
3. To develop ML models that give fast predictions of the plume footprint, travel distance, ring concentration and excursion chance, each with an uncertainty band.
4. To check whether contamination at ore depth could reach the shallow aquifer from which people drink.
5. To assess the measured groundwater quality against IS 10500:2012 block by block, keeping measured and modelled results separate.
6. To identify monitoring gaps and recommend where new monitoring is needed most.
7. To design an alert system that informs officials and residents, with an explanation and a recommended action for every alert.
8. To develop a prototype web portal with role-based access for administrators, regulators, analysts, field officers and residents.
9. To deploy the system online and test it thoroughly.
10. To state the system’s limits, so that a modelled estimate is never mistaken for a measurement and missing data is never shown as “safe”.

# 3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

Groundwater monitoring in India’s mining regions is mostly manual: samples are collected at intervals, tested in a laboratory and compared with limits. As the fellowship proposal pointed out, this reacts to problems after they happen instead of predicting them.

For ISR uranium mining the problem is sharper. ISR has never been carried out in India, so if it were ever considered for the Singhbhum belt, officials would have no tool to judge where contamination could go, how soon it would reach drinking-water wells, which villages would be affected, or where to place monitoring wells. The existing international experience comes from sandstone aquifers in the USA [@nureg1569; @usgs_tx], while Jharkhand’s uranium lies in fractured hard rock, where water and chemicals move very differently.

The existing monitoring data also has serious limits. The 2023 CGWB water-quality record for Jharkhand has only one sample per well [@cgwb_gwq]; the 2024 table adds a second year of general chemistry but no uranium or metals [@cgwb_gwq2024]. No well depth is recorded, iron, arsenic and manganese were never tested, and 55 wells in the uranium belt were never tested for uranium. With data like this, a purely data-driven ML model cannot forecast water-quality trends or show how a plume would spread, because nothing in the data ever spread. Most published groundwater ML studies need long records from many stations [@sahoo2013; @rajaee2019]. Standard vulnerability indices such as DRASTIC need soil, recharge and other layers that are not available at a useful scale for the state [@aller1987], and they answer a different question: they describe pollution from the surface, not from an injection deep underground.

Finally, even when a limit is crossed, a result that sits in a database table does not reach, or protect, the people who drink the water.

**Problem statement:** There is no tool for Jharkhand that can (a) estimate the spread of contamination from a hypothetical ISR operation in fractured hard rock using real local data, with honest uncertainty; (b) show what the existing groundwater quality record says, and what it does not say; (c) identify the places where monitoring is weakest; and (d) inform the officials and residents concerned through a working alert system. This project aims to build such a tool, and to be clear about which of these questions the available data can and cannot answer.
